STAGE 6 · DEEP LEARNING

Job readiness

Turn your skills and project evidence into a clear, credible interview and job-search process.

How to use this stage: read one lesson, reproduce the example without copying, explain the diagram aloud, then complete the exercise. Open the answer only after making an attempt.

LESSON 1 · 30–45 min

Resume: turn work into evidence

A resume is a short evidence document, not a list of technologies. Each bullet should help a reviewer understand the problem, your contribution and the result.

Structure and relevance

For an early-career developer, prioritize skills you can demonstrate, projects with working links, relevant experience and education. Keep formatting consistent and easy to scan. Tailor the top half to the role without inventing experience.

Bullet formula

Use action + scope + technical approach + outcome. Quantify only when you have a defensible measurement. If you cannot quantify impact, describe the concrete behavior delivered.

Weak: Made a website using React.
Stronger: Built a React task board with filtered views,
server-side validation and persistent PostgreSQL storage;
added integration tests for create/update permissions.

Project evidence

Link to a live demo and a clean repository. Pin your strongest repositories. Make sure the README works, setup steps are accurate, secrets are absent and the default branch is stable. Explain your own contribution if a project was collaborative.

Truth and clarity

Never claim a tool or skill you cannot explain. Be ready to discuss the design, one bug, one trade-off and what you would improve. Use a simple, readable format and proofread names, dates and links.

Practice task: Write three resume bullets for your capstone. For each, identify the evidence in the repository or demo that supports the claim.
Check your understanding

Question: What makes a project bullet credible?

Answer: A specific contribution, technical detail and verifiable outcome that you can explain.

Why: Concrete evidence is more useful than unsupported adjectives such as expert or innovative.

Interview practice

Question: Walk me through a project on your resume.

Strong answer should mention: Explain user problem, your role, architecture, hardest challenge, trade-off, test/deployment and measurable or observable outcome.

LESSON 2 · 30–45 min

Behavioral interviews and STAR stories

Behavioral questions explore how you work with other people, respond to uncertainty and learn from mistakes. Prepare real stories, but do not memorize a speech that sounds disconnected from the question.

STAR framework

Situation: enough context to understand the problem. Task: what you were responsible for. Action: the specific steps you took. Result: what changed and what you learned. Spend most of the answer on your actions and decisions.

Build a story bank

undefined

  • A difficult bug you investigated.
  • A disagreement or feedback you handled constructively.
  • A time you learned a new tool quickly.
  • A mistake and how you prevented a repeat.
  • A project where requirements changed.
  • A time you helped someone or asked for help.

Make the story specific

Use 'I' for your contribution and 'we' for team outcomes. Explain the options you considered and why you chose one. If the outcome was not successful, be honest about what you learned and what you changed afterward.

Practice task: Draft six STAR stories in bullet form. Practice each in 90 seconds, then ask a friend to identify the situation, your action and the result.
Check your understanding

Question: Which STAR section should usually contain the most detail?

Answer: Action, because it shows your decisions, ownership and problem-solving.

Why: Interviewers need to understand what you personally did, not only the team's situation.

Interview practice

Question: Tell me about a mistake you made.

Strong answer should mention: Choose a real, bounded mistake; own your part; explain impact, corrective action and the process change that reduced recurrence.

LESSON 3 · 30–45 min

Technical interviews: coding and problem solving

Interviewers evaluate how you reason, communicate and test—not only whether you remember a trick. A repeatable process makes your thinking visible.

A problem-solving routine

undefined

  • Restate the problem and clarify input/output constraints.
  • Work through a small example and edge cases.
  • Describe a straightforward solution first.
  • Analyze time and space complexity.
  • Improve only when the constraints require it.
  • Code in small steps and narrate decisions.
  • Test normal, boundary and adversarial cases.

Patterns to recognize

Hash maps help with counting and lookup; two pointers help with sorted sequences; sliding windows help with contiguous ranges; stacks help with nested or monotonic relationships; binary search helps with ordered or monotonic search spaces; BFS finds shortest paths in unweighted graphs; dynamic programming helps when subproblems overlap.

Communicate uncertainty

If stuck, say what you know, propose a small example and test a hypothesis. Ask a clarifying question when a requirement changes the algorithm. Do not silently switch assumptions.

After solving

State complexity, explain why the algorithm is correct, and mention a trade-off. If time remains, improve naming and tests rather than adding unnecessary abstractions.

Practice task: Complete one timed easy and one medium problem. Record where you spent time: understanding, algorithm choice, implementation or debugging. Repeat the weakest part.
Check your understanding

Question: Why test edge cases after writing the algorithm?

Answer: The main idea may be correct while implementation fails for empty input, duplicates, boundaries or overflow.

Why: Correctness includes all valid inputs, not just the example in the prompt.

Interview practice

Question: How do you approach a coding problem you have never seen?

Strong answer should mention: Clarify constraints, derive a brute-force baseline, identify a pattern, analyze complexity, implement incrementally and test edge cases.

LESSON 4 · 30–45 min

System design fundamentals

For junior roles, system design is often about clear requirements and sensible components rather than designing a global-scale platform. Explain the simplest architecture that meets the stated needs.

Clarify the requirements

Ask about users, core operations, read/write ratio, data size, latency, availability, privacy and expected growth. Separate essential requirements from assumptions. Estimate roughly rather than pretending to know exact traffic.

Core building blocks

A client communicates with an API; the API authenticates and authorizes; a database stores durable state; a cache reduces repeated reads; a queue handles asynchronous work; object storage holds large files. Add components only when a requirement justifies them.

Discuss bottlenecks

A slow endpoint may need a better query or index before a cache. A CPU-heavy task may move to a worker. A single database may be sufficient until measured load requires replicas or partitioning. Explain consistency and failure behavior for every added component.

Client → Load balancer → API instances
                         ├→ SQL database
                         ├→ Cache
                         └→ Queue → Worker → External service

Reliability and security

Discuss timeouts, bounded retries, idempotency, rate limiting, backups, monitoring, least privilege and data protection. Identify single points of failure and explain which failures the first version can tolerate.

Practice task: Design a URL shortener on paper: API endpoints, short-code generation, data model, redirect flow, abuse controls, caching strategy and failure cases.
Check your understanding

Question: Why should retries be bounded and use backoff?

Answer: Unbounded immediate retries can amplify an outage and overload a failing dependency.

Why: Backoff and jitter spread retry load; limits prevent endless work.

Interview practice

Question: How would you design a URL shortener?

Strong answer should mention: Cover create/redirect APIs, unique short-code generation, persistent mapping, cache, expiration/abuse controls, analytics trade-offs and availability.

LESSON 5 · 30–45 min

Job search, networking and continuous learning

A job search is a pipeline. Track applications and feedback, improve the weakest stage, and keep building evidence while you apply.

Target roles deliberately

Read several job descriptions for the same role and identify recurring requirements. Separate must-have skills from nice-to-have tools. Choose a small number of role types so your resume and preparation stay focused.

Application tracker

Record company, role, date, source, resume version, status, next action and notes. Follow up politely after a reasonable interval. Do not send identical mass messages; make a concise connection to the role or team's work.

Networking message

undefined

Hi [Name],
I’m learning backend development and recently built [specific project].
I noticed your work on [specific topic]. If you have 10 minutes,
I’d value one piece of advice on preparing for junior roles.
No worries if your schedule is full—thank you for considering it.

Improve the pipeline

If applications receive no replies, review role fit and resume evidence. If screens fail, practice explaining projects and fundamentals. If coding rounds fail, focus on problem-solving process and patterns. If final rounds fail, practice trade-offs, communication and behavioral stories.

Keep learning sustainably

Choose the next skill based on a project requirement or repeated interview gap. Build small experiments, write down what you learned and revisit it later. Avoid collecting courses without shipping work.

Practice task: Create a four-week application plan with target roles, weekly application goals, two mock interviews, one project improvement and a weekly review of conversion rates.
Check your understanding

Question: How can you diagnose a job-search bottleneck?

Answer: Track conversion between stages—applications, recruiter screens, technical rounds and offers—and improve the stage with the largest drop-off.

Why: A pipeline view replaces guesswork with evidence about what to practice next.

Interview practice

Question: How do you keep learning after joining a team?

Strong answer should mention: Use real work and feedback to identify gaps, set a focused learning goal, practice on a small task and share the result with teammates.

Stage project

Turn your skills and project evidence into a clear, credible interview and job-search process.

  1. Write a one-page scope and define the user problem.
  2. Build the smallest working version before adding extras.
  3. Test the happy path, invalid input, empty state and failure path.
  4. Write a README with setup steps, screenshots and trade-offs.
  5. Ask someone to use it and record what confused them.

Definition of done: a stranger can run it, understand its purpose, and verify its main behavior.
